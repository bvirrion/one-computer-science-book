The old lighthouse stands at the end of the harbour wall, where the river meets the sea.
For more than a hundred years its keepers climbed the stairs every evening to light the
lamp, and every morning they came down to write in the logbook. They wrote the weather,
the ships that passed, the time the lamp was lit and the time it was put out. When a
storm came in from the west, the keeper stayed awake all night, watching the light turn
and listening to the waves break against the rocks below.

The logbooks are kept today in a small room of the town museum. Anyone can read them.
The handwriting changes every few years, when a new keeper arrived, but the order of
the entries never changes: the date, the wind, the sea, the ships. One keeper drew
little pictures of the boats in the margin. Another wrote short notes about the birds
that rested on the gallery during the long winter nights.

Today the lamp turns on by itself when the evening grows dark, and a computer in the
harbour office checks that it is working. Nobody climbs the stairs at night any more.
But on the first Sunday of every month, the museum opens the door at the foot of the
tower, and visitors climb the one hundred and twelve steps to the top. From the
gallery they can see the whole town, the river winding inland between the fields, and
on a clear day the hills on the other side of the bay.

The children who visit like to count the steps out loud. They like the great glass lens
even more: it is taller than they are, and it bends the light of a single small lamp
into a beam that sailors could see from far out at sea. The guide explains that each
lighthouse along the coast has its own rhythm of flashes, so that a sailor who sees a
light at night can tell which one it is, and know where the ship is. Three short
flashes and a long dark pause means this harbour; two flashes means the rocks to the
north; a steady light means the river mouth.
